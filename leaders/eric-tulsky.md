# Eric Tulsky — Research Guide

Eric Tulsky is especially useful to study because his public hockey-analytics work shows how analytical progress can come from **asking a better question, choosing a better unit of analysis, and collecting the missing data needed to answer it**.

Before joining an NHL front office, Tulsky published a large body of applied hockey analysis at Broad Street Hockey and related outlets. Much of it was not written as conventional academic research. Instead, it took the form of short, iterative studies: identify a hockey claim, decide what observable evidence should exist if the claim is true, test it, revise the metric when needed, and communicate the practical implication.

The most important public research program is his work on **zone entries and neutral-zone play**, culminating in the 2013 MIT Sloan Sports Analytics Conference paper *Using Zone Entry Data To Separate Offensive, Neutral, And Defensive Zone Performance*, written with Geoffrey Detweiler, Robert Spencer, and Corey Sznajder.

> **Scope note:** unlike Luke Bornn, Tulsky does not have a large public academic sports-research bibliography. This guide therefore treats his substantive public hockey analyses as research artifacts when they contain a clear question, data, method, and inference. It prioritizes work that teaches a reusable analytical idea rather than attempting to catalog every article he wrote. His private work after entering the NHL is necessarily outside the scope of a public research inventory.

## Seven-pillar legend

- **M** — Measurement
- **P** — Prediction
- **V** — Player valuation
- **C** — Causal inference
- **S** — State & value modeling
- **D** — Decision science
- **T** — Spatial & tracking analytics

## The recurring ideas in Tulsky's work

### 1. Fix the measurement problem before reaching for a more complicated model

A recurring Tulsky move is to ask whether the standard statistic is even measuring the phenomenon people think it measures. Goals contain enormous shooting and save-percentage noise. Corsi is affected by deployment. Shots per game confound possession generation with what happens after possession is established. Team-level outcomes hide where on the ice the advantage was actually created.

His work repeatedly improves the denominator or decomposes the process before adding complexity.

### 2. Context is part of player evaluation

Zone starts, quality of competition, teammates, game state, and role all affect observed results. Tulsky's early work on Balanced Zone Shifts and Balanced Corsi is an important precursor to the broader player-valuation principle that raw on-ice outcomes should not be interpreted independently of usage.

### 3. Repeatability matters more than descriptive impressiveness

A statistic can describe what happened without measuring a stable skill. Tulsky repeatedly asks whether an apparent effect persists, regresses, or predicts future results. This appears in work on shooting percentage, special teams, goaltending, zone performance, and individual entry behavior.

### 4. If the available data cannot answer the question, collect new data

The zone-entry project is the clearest example. NHL play-by-play did not record how teams crossed the blue line, so the project manually tracked entries and merged them with play-by-play outcomes. That one additional event definition made it possible to separate neutral-zone performance from offensive- and defensive-zone performance.

### 5. The useful endpoint is a hockey decision

Tulsky's work rarely stops at a metric. The question is usually what the result means for tactics, deployment, contracts, roster construction, draft trades, or evaluation. That makes his public work especially strong as a model of applied decision support.

# Research inventory

## Measurement, usage, and player evaluation

### Balanced Zone Shifts: the evolution of a new statistic — 2011

**Source:** Broad Street Hockey — [article](https://www.broadstreethockey.com/post/balanced-zone-shifts/)

**What it does:** Examines whether players move play from the defensive end toward the offensive end by comparing where their shifts begin and end, while explicitly recognizing that coaches strongly control starting position.

**Why it matters:** A simple difference between offensive-zone start percentage and finish percentage can look like player impact even when much of the starting distribution is externally assigned. The work illustrates the need to distinguish player behavior from deployment.

**Pillars:** **M, V**

**Repository connection:** This is the same conceptual problem faced by modern RAPM, contextualized rate metrics, and role-adjusted player models: observed performance is partly generated by where and with whom a player is used.

---

### Balanced Corsi: Who is best at their role? — 2011

**Source:** Broad Street Hockey — [article](https://www.broadstreethockey.com/post/balanced-corsi/)

**What it does:** Adjusts interpretation of on-ice shot-attempt differential by comparing players to others with similar usage, particularly zone starts and competition.

**Why it matters:** It makes an early and important distinction between **performance** and **performance given role**. A player beginning many shifts in the defensive zone against strong opponents should not be evaluated as though the environment were neutral.

**Pillars:** **M, V**

**Repository connection:** This is a useful conceptual predecessor to adjusted plus-minus thinking. The exact adjustment method is less important than the reasoning: deployment is part of the data-generating process.

---

### Young forwards and tough defensive minutes — 2011

**Source:** Broad Street Hockey — [article](https://www.broadstreethockey.com/post/schenn-couturier-defense-two-way-forwards/)

**What it does:** Defines difficult minutes through defensive-zone starts and quality of competition, then uses those measures to examine the roles given to young forwards.

**Why it matters:** Evaluation should distinguish what a player produced from what he was asked to do. Usage itself can also contain information about how a coaching staff perceives a player's skills.

**Pillars:** **M, V**

**Repository connection:** A reminder that role variables can be both confounders and signals. Coaches' deployment choices may reflect information about the player while simultaneously affecting the player's observed results.

---

### Sean Couturier's continued evolution as an elite defensive player — 2013

**Source:** Broad Street Hockey — [article](https://www.broadstreethockey.com/post/sean-couturier-stats-defense-flyers/)

**What it does:** Evaluates Couturier's results in the context of unusually difficult competition and defensive-zone deployment, while explicitly warning about small samples.

**Why it matters:** The article is a compact example of contextual player evaluation: raw outcomes, usage, competition, and uncertainty need to be read together.

**Pillars:** **M, V**

## Reliability, regression, and prediction

### An updated look at the Flyers shooting percentage — 2011

**Source:** Broad Street Hockey — [article](https://www.broadstreethockey.com/post/why-i-love-regression-to-the-mean/)

**What it does:** Compares unusually high current shooting percentages with players' longer-term histories and checks what happened afterward.

**Why it matters:** It is a simple demonstration of regression to the mean and the distinction between current outcome and underlying talent. Extreme short-run percentages should not automatically be projected forward.

**Pillars:** **M, P, V**

**Repository connection:** This is the intuitive foundation for shrinkage, partial pooling, reliability thresholds, and Bayesian player estimates.

---

### What makes a good special teams unit? — 2011

**Source:** Broad Street Hockey — [article](https://www.broadstreethockey.com/post/what-makes-a-good-special-teams-unit/)

**What it does:** Re-examines whether shot rates or conversion percentages are the better basis for evaluating power plays and penalty kills, and publicly revises an earlier conclusion when new evidence changes the answer.

**Why it matters:** The methodological lesson is larger than special teams: choose metrics based on repeatability and predictive usefulness, not just because they correlate with the outcome retrospectively.

**Pillars:** **M, P**

**Repository connection:** An excellent example of model defense. A metric should earn trust by predicting or stabilizing, and analysts should update their position when validation contradicts the original argument.

---

### Do Playmakers Drive Teammates' Shooting Percentage? — 2011

**Source:** Broad Street Hockey — [article](https://www.broadstreethockey.com/post/do-playmakers-drive-teammates-shooting-percentage/)

**What it does:** Asks whether elite playmakers systematically increase teammates' shooting percentages by creating better opportunities, comparing teammates' performance with and without the playmaker.

**Why it matters:** This is an early attempt to isolate an indirect player effect that box-score assists do not fully capture. It also highlights the statistical difficulty of teammate effects and WOWY-style comparisons.

**Pillars:** **M, V, C**

**Repository connection:** The causal question is compelling—does playing with a particular creator improve shot conversion?—but observational with/without comparisons can be confounded by linemates, deployment, opponents, and selection. It is therefore a good bridge from player valuation into causal-inference thinking.

---

### This year's Flyers: sorting fact from fiction — 2011

**Source:** Broad Street Hockey — [article](https://www.broadstreethockey.com/post/this-years-flyers-sorting-fact-from-fiction/)

**What it does:** Separates noisy outcome metrics from more stable process measures when evaluating special teams and team performance.

**Why it matters:** It reinforces a core sports-analytics distinction: the metric closest to the scoreboard is not necessarily the best measure of underlying ability, particularly in small samples.

**Pillars:** **M, P**

## Zone entries and the neutral-zone research program

### Zone Entry Article Archive / initial tracking studies — 2011–2012

**Source:** Broad Street Hockey — [archive](https://www.broadstreethockey.com/post/zone-entry-article-archive/)

**What it does:** Develops a new manual data set recording who sends the puck into the offensive zone and whether the entry is controlled or uncontrolled, then progressively studies team results, individual contributions, entry outcomes, counterattacks, scoring chances, reliability, and on-ice effects.

**Why it matters:** This is a model for applied research iteration. The project does not begin with a sophisticated model. It begins with a missing event that matters tactically, collects it consistently, links it to downstream outcomes, tests candidate metrics, and expands only as the evidence supports doing so.

**Pillars:** **M, P, V, S, D**

**Repository connection:** This may be the single most important Tulsky research program for the seven-pillar framework. It demonstrates how **measurement design creates the possibility of later valuation and decision science**.

---

### Flyers zone entries: winning the neutral zone — 2011

**Source:** Broad Street Hockey — [article](https://www.broadstreethockey.com/post/philadelphia-flyers-zone-entries/)

**What it does:** Separates team performance into neutral-zone success, offensive-zone production following entries, and defensive-zone suppression following entries.

**Why it matters:** Aggregate shot differential does not tell us *where* a team is winning. Zone-entry data provides an intermediate state that makes process decomposition possible.

**Pillars:** **M, S, V**

**Repository connection:** This is directly relevant to the repo's developing work on entry defense. Instead of treating an entry attempt as an isolated event, the framework can ask who prevented the entry, how the entry occurred, and what downstream value followed when prevention failed.

---

### Flyers Zone Entries, Games 1–22 — 2011–2012

**Source:** Broad Street Hockey — [article](https://www.broadstreethockey.com/post/philadelphia-flyers-zone-entries-642adb72f572b5e433073fd8/)

**What it does:** Uses a larger sample to separate entry quantity, controlled-entry rate, on-ice neutral-zone results, and downstream shot generation.

**Why it matters:** It illustrates how the same data supports multiple levels of analysis: individual puck-handling skill, on-ice territorial impact, team strategy, and downstream offensive value.

**Pillars:** **M, V, S**

---

### Using Zone Entry Data To Separate Offensive, Neutral, And Defensive Zone Performance — 2013

**Authors:** Eric Tulsky, Geoffrey Detweiler, Robert Spencer, Corey Sznajder  
**Source:** MIT Sloan Sports Analytics Conference Research Paper Competition — [paper](https://hockeyanalytics.com/Research_files/Using%20Zone%20Entry%20Data%20To%20Separate%20Offensive%2C%20Neutral%2C%20And%20Defensive%20Zone%20Performance.pdf)

**What it does:** Hand-tracks zone entries across more than 300 NHL games, merges them with official play-by-play, and partitions shot differential into performance in the offensive, neutral, and defensive zones. The paper also compares controlled and uncontrolled entries and studies which components appear to distinguish players and teams.

**Major finding:** Controlled entries produced more than twice as many shots, scoring chances, and goals as dump-ins in the sample. The analysis also found that team shot differential was driven heavily by neutral-zone success rather than large, readily detectable differences in shots generated or suppressed per offensive-zone possession.

**Why it matters:** This work changes the denominator from **shots per game** to **shots per possession/entry**. That single change separates the ability to create possessions from the ability to produce once possession has been established.

**Pillars:** **M, P, V, S, D**

**Repository connection:** This paper is a blueprint for decomposing a complex outcome into stages:

**defensive-zone exit → neutral-zone advancement → entry attempt → entry outcome → offensive-zone possession → shot quality / scoring**

Each stage can have its own expected value and its own player credit assignment. This is highly relevant to building richer entry-stop metrics rather than simply counting successful denials.

---

### Neutral Zone Score / expected Fenwick from entry outcomes — 2012–2013

**Sources:** Zone-entry research program and 2013 Sloan paper

**What it does:** Assigns average downstream shot value to different entry types and asks what shot share would be expected based purely on a team's or player's neutral-zone results.

**Why it matters:** This is a primitive but powerful **expected-value model**. Instead of counting every entry equally, the model weights states by their empirically observed downstream consequences.

**Pillars:** **M, V, S**

**Repository connection:** Conceptually, this sits on the same continuum as EPA and EPV. An event receives value because it changes the distribution of future outcomes.

## Roster, draft, and decision analysis

### Should Scott Hartnell get a contract extension? — 2012

**Source:** Broad Street Hockey

**What it does:** Evaluates whether a 30-year-old coming off a 37-goal season should receive a long extension, decomposing the apparent breakout into shooting rate, shooting percentage, role, and sustainability rather than simply extrapolating goals.

**Why it matters:** Contract decisions require forecasts of future talent, not descriptions of the most recent season. The useful question is which components of performance changed and which are likely to persist.

**Pillars:** **M, P, V, D**

**Repository connection:** A clean example of converting descriptive player metrics into a roster decision under aging and performance uncertainty.

---

### NHL draft: What does it cost to trade up? — 2013

**Source:** Broad Street Hockey — [article](https://www.broadstreethockey.com/post/nhl-draft-pick-value-trading-up/)

**What it does:** Estimates the **market exchange value** of NHL draft picks from actual pick-for-pick trades between 2006 and 2012. Rather than estimating what picks *should* be worth from future player outcomes, Tulsky fits a function describing what teams historically paid to move between draft positions.

**Why it matters:** It distinguishes **fundamental value** from **market price**. Those are different estimands and should not be conflated.

**Pillars:** **M, P, D**

**Repository connection:** This distinction is central to front-office analytics. A team can estimate both the expected player value of a pick and the market's exchange rate, then look for situations where the two disagree.

---

### The ups and downs of the Flyers schedule — 2013

**Source:** Broad Street Hockey — [article](https://www.broadstreethockey.com/post/2013-flyers-schedule-analysis/)

**What it does:** Applies previously estimated effects of rest to the upcoming schedule to quantify how much advantage or disadvantage the distribution of rest days creates.

**Why it matters:** A statistically real contextual effect does not automatically have a large practical impact. Tulsky translates the effect into expected wins/points and finds the schedule-level consequence to be modest.

**Pillars:** **P, D**

**Repository connection:** Always translate model coefficients into the scale of the sports decision. Statistical significance and practical importance are not the same thing.

## Goaltending, narrative, and attribution

### Does Flyers goalie coach Jeff Reese deserve more criticism? — 2013

**Source:** Broad Street Hockey — [article](https://www.broadstreethockey.com/post/jeff-reese-goalie-coach-flyers-criticism/)

**What it does:** Challenges narrative attribution around a goalie coach by examining whether the observed performance of goalies under that coach provides evidence for the claimed developmental effect.

**Why it matters:** Attribution is difficult when player talent, aging, team environment, selection, and random variance all move simultaneously. A plausible story is not the same as evidence of causal impact.

**Pillars:** **M, V, C**

**Repository connection:** Useful as an example of the limits of observational causal claims. Coaching effects are especially susceptible to selection bias and small samples.

---

### Is it harder to score goals in the NHL playoffs? — 2012

**Source:** Broad Street Hockey — [article](https://www.broadstreethockey.com/post/nhl-playoff-scoring/)

**What it does:** Tests whether scoring actually becomes harder in the playoffs while recognizing that playoff participants are not a random sample of NHL teams.

**Why it matters:** A raw regular-season versus playoff comparison is confounded by selection: better teams and players disproportionately reach the playoffs. The relevant baseline needs to reflect who is participating.

**Pillars:** **M, P, C**

**Repository connection:** This is a simple introduction to counterfactual thinking. The question is not merely whether playoff scoring is lower; it is how the same caliber of teams/players would be expected to score under regular-season conditions.

# How Tulsky maps to the seven pillars

| Pillar | Strength in public work | Representative examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Measurement** | **Very strong** | Zone entries, Corsi context, usage, special-teams measurement, new denominators |
| **Prediction** | **Strong** | Regression to mean, metric repeatability, player/contract sustainability, schedule effects |
| **Player valuation** | **Very strong** | Balanced Corsi, tough minutes, individual entry skills, context-aware evaluation |
| **Causal inference** | **Moderate / mostly implicit** | Playmaker effects, coaching attribution, playoff environment; often exposes why naive causal claims are difficult |
| **State & value modeling** | **Strong** | Entry states, shots per possession, Neutral Zone Score, downstream value of controlled entries |
| **Decision science** | **Strong** | Carry vs. dump strategy, draft-pick market value, contracts, schedule magnitude |
| **Spatial & tracking analytics** | **Limited by modern standards** | Zone location is central, but the public work predates widespread NHL player/puck tracking and is event-tracking rather than full spatiotemporal modeling |

This distribution is important. Tulsky's public work should not be retroactively described as a modern player-tracking portfolio. Its major innovation was often **creating a meaningful event-level data set and decomposing hockey into better states**, not modeling continuous x-y trajectories.

# The Tulsky analytical workflow

A useful abstraction of his public approach is:

1. **Start with a hockey claim or decision.**  
   Is dump-and-chase effective? Is this player really driving possession? Is a special-teams unit actually good? What does it cost to trade up?

2. **Ask what the current statistic confounds.**  
   Usage? Luck? Possession count? Teammate effects? Selection? Market value versus intrinsic value?

3. **Find or create a better unit of analysis.**  
   Shift starts, shot attempts, zone entries, offensive-zone possessions, controlled versus uncontrolled entries, historical pick trades.

4. **Check repeatability or downstream consequence.**  
   Does the metric persist? Does it predict future results? What happens after the event?

5. **Separate process components.**  
   Neutral zone versus offensive zone; possession creation versus production within possession; shooting volume versus shooting percentage.

6. **Translate the result into hockey language.**  
   Carry the puck more. Reconsider how a player is being evaluated. Do not overreact to a hot percentage. Understand the market cost of a draft move.

7. **Revise when the evidence changes.**  
   The special-teams article is particularly instructive because Tulsky explicitly changes his earlier view after seeing better evidence.

# What an applied analyst should learn from Tulsky

## 1. Better data often beats a fancier model

The zone-entry project is a canonical example. No advanced algorithm could recover controlled-entry behavior from a data set that never recorded it. Creating one well-defined event changed the questions hockey analysts could answer.

## 2. Choose the denominator that matches the mechanism

Shots allowed per game combines how often the opponent gets possession with what happens after it does. Shots per offensive-zone possession isolates a different skill. Many analytical breakthroughs are denominator breakthroughs.

## 3. Decompose the game before assigning credit

If a team has a strong shot differential, ask where it came from. If a defender suppresses shots, ask whether the effect came from defensive-zone play, preventing entries, or keeping the puck at the other end.

## 4. Stability is evidence about skill

When a metric is noisy, ask what persists across halves, seasons, players, or contexts. Descriptive accuracy alone does not make a statistic a good talent estimate.

## 5. Value intermediate actions by what happens next

A controlled entry matters because of the future offense it creates. That principle generalizes naturally to exits, recoveries, passes, pressure, and other micro-events.

## 6. Distinguish prediction from causation

A with/without effect may help forecast performance without proving that the player caused the difference. Tulsky's public work often raises questions that modern causal methods could attack more explicitly.

## 7. Keep the decision attached to the analysis

The most memorable Tulsky studies are easy to state in hockey terms because the analytical endpoint matters operationally: carry rather than dump when possible; adjust evaluation for deployment; distrust unsustainable percentages; price a draft move against the actual market.

# Direct connection to entry-defense research

Tulsky's zone-entry work provides a particularly useful foundation for studying **defensive entry stops**.

The natural extension is not simply:

**Did the opponent enter the zone?**

It is:

**Given the attacking context, how much did each defender change the probability distribution over entry outcomes and downstream possession value?**

That suggests a progression such as:

1. Define the unit as an **opponent zone-entry attempt**.
2. Classify the outcome: controlled entry, uncontrolled entry, denial/turnover, forced regroup, icing, etc.
3. Identify defender roles: puck-carrier pressure, pass-option pressure, gap controller, support defender, recovery player.
4. Estimate expected outcome/value from context before assigning player credit.
5. Measure the difference between expected and actual entry outcome/value.
6. Adjust longer-run player estimates for teammates, opponents, systems, score state, and sample size.
7. Use the metric as a **flag for scouting/video review**, not as automatic proof of individual defensive quality.

This preserves the core Tulsky insight—**separate the stages of play and value them with the right denominator**—while extending it into a richer modern player-valuation problem.
